in the dynamics of complex systems, yaneer-bar-yam introduces the phenomenon of emergent complexity.

which is: 

---

any system composed of simple parts where the collective behaviour is complex. 

---

which means that the outcome can't be determined simply by studying the individual parts. 

---

a popular example in nature is the formation of dendrites in snowflakes. 

---

you can think of them as starting from the center, and following a system of simple rules, but producing unpredictable forms because of the order of execution for those rules, and their impact of the execution of one rule over the other. 

---

a simpler example to comprehend is any social group. there are rules between each set of actors within the group, and then global rules for the group itself; making it very difficult to predict how the different rules & actors may interact with each other in different conditions.

---

the simplest example of a computational system that exhibits emergent complexity is one-dimensional cellular automata.

imagine if i define a cell, capable of expressing one of two states: 0 / or dead / or off. 

---

and 1 / or alive / or on.

---

and i form a row of them. here, i have a row of cells with alternating states. the first one is zero, the second is one 1 and so that pattern repeats. 

---

for generating the next row, i can define a ruleset. 

where each cell looks at its neighbors from the previous generation to determine its state for the next. 

here, for example, a cell that had the same neighbors in the previous generation (whether 0 or 1) will have a state of 1.

stephen wolfram — in his book a new kind of science — has exhaustively studied permutations of such rulesets & starting configurations.

---

however, what's interesting to me is that a cell's state of 0 or 1 does not need to just be black or white.

---

in fact, it can be whatever you want.

---

for example, here i represent 0 as a square, and 1 as a circle.

---

they don't even need to be distinct shapes at all. 

here, i represent the state as a rotated line segment. notice how a more distinct pattern emerges, simply by representing the state a little differently.

---

the representation does not have to be uniform either. 

here, line segments closer to the center are weaker than the ones away from it.

---

they also don't need to be visual at all.

here, 0 is represented by the text "hi"; while 1 is represented by the text "bye". therefore, each line is some combination of either of these words. 

---

point being: that the ruleset for an emergent system can afford more than just its representation. 

---

with the same underlying base-system, one can produce — what i'll call — 'software-interpretations'.

---

now this isn't new. instructional artists like sol lewitt have been using this for years. 

---

this is wall drawing number 392, where a space is subdivided into squares, with each square being bisected by a diagonal line.

however, the direction of lines in each square is left to the artist who executes the system. 

---

therefore, two artists might produce a wholly different interpretation. 

---

casey reas is a software artist who explored this premise in process compendium. 

he defines a system by combining simple forms such as a circle & a line, and simple behaviours such as move in a straight line.

combinations of these result in what he calls 'elements'. 

---

showing just the elements, however, would not be artistically interesting. therefore, he interprets them — exposing different relationships from the base system that he finds interesting. for example, here, he draws quadrilaterals between the endpoints of each pair of elements that touch each other.

---

that produces imagery like this. 

---

what bothers me about systems like these is that the creation rules is so arbitrary.
